## Chapter 5 — Concrete: pre-pour, during-pour, post-pour

Pre-Pour Inspection: The Hold Point That Matters Most

The pre-pour reinforcement inspection is a contractual hold point. Work may not proceed — concrete may not be placed — until the inspector has completed the pre-pour inspection and issued written concurrence. This is not a suggestion. If concrete is placed before the pre-pour inspection is complete, the work must be rejected in its entirety. The cost and delay of removing improperly poured concrete, re-fabricating formwork, and replacing reinforcement is the contractor's responsibility and serves as a very effective motivation for future compliance.

Before requesting the pre-pour inspection, the contractor should have in hand an approved rebar submittal (shop drawings and material certifications), approved formwork drawings (for significant pours), and approved embedded items submittals for any anchors, sleeves, conduit, or other items to be cast in place. The inspector verifies conformance of the actual installation to those approved submittals and to the structural drawings and specifications.

Rebar size and spacing are verified by count and measurement. Count the bars in every row and column of each rebar mat. Verify spacing — the structural drawings will typically show a dimension like "No. 6 bars at 12 inches on center each way." Measure the actual spacing in at least three locations across the element. Do not accept "close enough" — a specification that says 12 inches means 12 inches, with a tolerance typically defined by ACI 318 as plus or minus 3 inches for d (effective depth) in flexural members and plus or minus 1/2 inch for clear cover. Measure the actual spacing.

Concrete cover — the distance from the outermost surface of the reinforcement to the face of the concrete — is critical to the durability of reinforced concrete structures. ACI 318 specifies minimum concrete cover requirements by exposure condition: 1.5 inches for interior slabs and walls not exposed to weather; 2 inches for exposed-to-weather elements for bars No. 5 and smaller; 3 inches for elements cast against and permanently exposed to earth. Industrial construction frequently involves more aggressive cover requirements for chemical resistance or extreme environments — confirm the project specification. Verify cover using a plastic or wooden spacer ("chair") or by direct measurement with a ruler to the form face. Wire chairs, plastic chairs, and slab bolsters are the standard support method for bottom mat rebar in slabs; they must be rated for the load of the rebar and the construction traffic.

Check the rebar for cleanliness. Concrete cannot bond to rebar that is coated with mud, oil, form release agent, or heavy rust scale. Light rust (surface oxidation without pitting or scale) is acceptable and may actually improve bond. Heavy rust scale that flakes off when wire-brushed reduces the effective bar diameter and must be cleaned or the bar replaced. Mud-coated bars are a rejection — they require cleaning before concrete is placed.

Embedded items require careful inspection. Every anchor bolt, conduit sleeve, pipe sleeve, construction joint key, and blockout must be verified against the structural drawings for location, elevation, and orientation. Check anchor bolt patterns with a template cut from plywood or measured from a base plate layout drawing — bolt group patterns must be within the tolerance specified on the drawings, typically plus or minus 1/8 inch from true position. Verify that sleeves are capped or otherwise protected from concrete intrusion. Verify that conduit and pipes do not interrupt required rebar and do not reduce the concrete section below the minimum specified in ACI 318. The code is specific: pipes and conduit embedded in concrete must not exceed one-third of the slab thickness in outside diameter, and spacing between conduit items must be at least three diameters.

Formwork inspection addresses tightness, bracing adequacy, and cleanliness. Look for gaps at form joints where paste can run out — a gap of more than 1/16 inch will allow paste bleed that leaves a rough surface and may indicate inadequate form bearing. Check bracing for kicker sizes and attachment to deadmen or stable anchor points. A form failure during a concrete pour is a life-safety emergency. Verify that the inside of the form is clean — no standing water, no debris, no ice. If the specification requires form release agent, verify it has been applied. Verify that all forms are wet-proofed or oiled as required for stripping without damage to the concrete surface.

Notify the engineer of record when the pre-pour inspection is complete. Many specifications require the engineer to be present for the pre-pour inspection of significant structural elements. If the inspector has findings that must be corrected before pouring, issue a written list to the contractor's superintendent and re-inspect after corrections are made. Do not issue verbal pre-pour approval — the authorization to pour must be in writing.

During-Pour Monitoring: The Tests That Define the Batch

Concrete testing during placement is not a bureaucratic obligation. It is the only way to know what material was placed in that element. The test cylinders will eventually tell you whether the concrete achieved design compressive strength. The slump, air content, temperature, and unit weight tests conducted at the truck tell you whether the concrete placed that day was conforming at the time of placement. Both are required. One without the other is incomplete.

The concrete testing frequency is established by the specification — typically one set of tests per 50 cubic yards or per half-day's pour, plus one set for the first and last truck of each day. A test set consists of slump, air content (for air-entrained concrete), unit weight, temperature, and a minimum of four strength cylinders (two tested at 7 days and two at 28 days, or as required by the specification).

Slump testing per ASTM C143 measures the workability of fresh concrete — roughly, how much water is in the mix relative to the cementitious material. The slump cone is filled in three layers, each rodded 25 times with a standard tamping rod. After filling and leveling, the cone is lifted vertically in 5 to 6 seconds. The slump is measured as the vertical distance the concrete settles from the top of the cone to the top of the settled concrete mass. Maximum slump is specified by the contract to control water-cement ratio — a slump above the specification maximum indicates excess water has been added, which reduces strength and durability. Minimum slump is specified (or implied by placement requirements) to ensure the concrete is workable enough for proper consolidation.

The most common field slump failure results from water added to the truck at the site. Drivers add water to "juice up" a stiff mix that has been sitting in the drum on a hot day or stuck in traffic. This is a code violation (ACI 301 limits the water-cement ratio of the mix as placed) and a specification violation. If the slump of a truck exceeds the specification maximum, the load must be rejected. The truck returns to the plant. Do not allow the contractor to "blend" a high-slump load with a low-slump load in the receiving pump or placement equipment to average out the problem — this is an unacceptable practice and cannot be verified.

Air content testing per ASTM C231 uses a pressure meter to measure the volume of air entrained in the fresh concrete. Air entrainment is required in concrete exposed to freeze-thaw cycling — the microscopic air bubbles provide relief space for the expansion of freezing water, preventing scaling and surface deterioration. Specified air content for exterior concrete in freeze-thaw regions is typically 5 to 8%, varying with aggregate size. Verify the project specification. The air content test is also critical for concrete poured in hot weather — slump adjusting admixtures can unpredictably affect air content, and a mix that tested properly at the plant may arrive on site with significantly different air content after drum rotation in hot temperatures.

Unit weight (density) testing per ASTM C138 provides a cross-check on air content and aggregate proportions. The unit weight of a mix with known aggregate and cement proportions is predictable from the mix design. A significantly lower unit weight than expected indicates more air (or a lighter aggregate mix) than intended. A significantly higher unit weight indicates inadequate air entrainment. The inspector should have the expected unit weight from the approved mix design and compare to measured values.

Temperature measurement per ASTM C1064 requires a calibrated thermometer inserted at least 4 inches into the fresh concrete within 5 minutes of obtaining the sample. ACI 305R (Hot Weather Concreting) and the specification typically establish maximum placing temperature of 90°F (32°C); ACI 306R (Cold Weather Concreting) establishes minimum placing temperature of 50°F for sections greater than 12 inches and 55°F for thinner sections. These limits exist because temperature profoundly affects hydration rate, set time, and strength development. Concrete placed too hot will experience accelerated setting, reduced ultimate strength, and increased cracking. Concrete placed too cold will experience delayed or arrested setting and inadequate strength gain during the critical early period. Both conditions require special procedures.

Strength cylinders are cast per ASTM C31. The mold is filled in three equal layers, each rodded 25 times (for cylinders up to 4-inch diameter) or each vibrated using an internal vibrator (for larger cylinders or for concrete too stiff to rod). The cylinder is capped, labeled with the date, pour location, truck number, and test set number, wrapped or covered to prevent moisture loss, and left to cure undisturbed for 24 hours before transport. Initial curing must occur at temperatures between 60°F and 80°F — this is a frequent violation in both winter and summer, where cylinders are left in the open on a cold night or in a heated trailer that exceeds 80°F. The inspector is responsible for the initial curing conditions of the cylinders. Bring an insulated cylinder curing box to every winter pour. In hot weather, store cylinders in a shaded, insulated location away from direct sun.

Cylinders are tested per ASTM C39 — one at 7 days and one at 28 days per standard practice (the 7-day result is indicative but not binding; the 28-day result is the compliance test). If the 28-day result falls below the specified f'c, the protocol depends on the specification and ACI 318 Section 26.12: additional cores from the structure may be taken; the engineer may accept the in-place concrete if the measured f'c of cores meets the ACI acceptance criteria; or the engineer may require structural analysis and potentially remediation. The core test is the definitive evaluation of in-place strength.

Concrete placement requirements deserve explicit attention. ACI 318 prohibits free-fall placement from heights exceeding 5 feet for most applications — concrete dropped more than 5 feet segregates, separating the heavy coarse aggregate from the paste and producing honeycombed zones in the lower portion of the pour. Verify that pump lines, tremies, and chutes are positioned to place concrete within this limit. During wall pours, the contractor should use a flexible trunk hose (elephant trunk) attached to the pump boom to direct concrete to the bottom of the wall form and raise it progressively — not dump from the top of a 12-foot wall form.

Vibrator operation is the key to consolidating fresh concrete and eliminating voids. Internal vibrators (pencil vibrators) should be inserted vertically at regular intervals not exceeding 1.5 times the vibrator's radius of action — typically 18 to 24 inches between insertions for a 2-inch vibrator. Each insertion should penetrate the previously placed lift to a depth of 6 inches to knit the lifts together. The vibrator should be slowly withdrawn — approximately 3 inches per second — to allow air and voids to close behind it. Inadequate vibration produces honeycombing; excessive vibration in one spot produces segregation. Watch the vibrator operator: a skilled operator works methodically in a pattern; an unskilled one randomly dips the vibrator in the same spot repeatedly.

Post-Pour: Curing, Protection, and Hot and Cold Weather Special Requirements

Concrete strength gains through the chemical process of hydration — cement reacting with water to form the crystalline calcium silicate hydrate (C-S-H) structure that provides concrete its strength. Hydration requires both water and temperature. If the concrete dries out prematurely, hydration stops and the concrete will never achieve its design strength. If the concrete freezes before reaching approximately 500 psi, the ice crystals rupture the nascent C-S-H matrix, permanently reducing strength. Curing is the practice of maintaining adequate moisture and temperature in the fresh concrete for sufficient time to achieve the specified strength and durability.

ACI 308 (Guide to External Curing of Concrete) specifies minimum curing durations. For conventional portland cement concrete (Type I or II cement), a minimum of 7 days of curing is required for ambient temperatures above 50°F. Concrete with Type III high-early-strength cement may meet strength requirements in 3 days, but durability properties (permeability, surface hardness, abrasion resistance) still benefit from extended curing. For concrete placed on industrial projects where durability is paramount — chemical containment floors, exterior flatwork in freeze-thaw environments, equipment pads subject to chemical spill — the specification may call for 14 or 28 days of wet curing.

Curing methods include: wet burlap covered with polyethylene sheeting (highly effective, labor-intensive); liquid curing compound sprayed on the surface immediately after finishing (easy, but must be removed if a bonded overlay or coating will be applied later); curing blankets (insulating blankets for cold weather that provide both moisture retention and temperature control); and ponding or continuous water application (most effective for flat work). Verify that the curing method is applied immediately after finishing operations on horizontal surfaces — within 30 minutes of completion. Concrete left uncured on a hot, windy day will begin to crack from plastic shrinkage before it even reaches initial set.

Hot weather concreting (ACI 305R) is defined as conditions where concrete is at risk of too-rapid setting due to high ambient temperature, high concrete temperature, direct sun, or high wind. Conditions that individually or collectively trigger hot weather procedures include ambient temperature above 90°F, direct sun exposure, relative humidity below 40%, and wind speed above 15 mph. Required measures include: pre-cooling water (using chilled water or ice as part of the mix water); using fly ash or slag as partial cement replacement to reduce heat of hydration; chilling aggregates; covering trucks to reduce heat gain in transit; using retarding admixtures to extend set time; scheduling pours for early morning or evening; and providing windbreaks, sunshades, and fog mist systems at the placement location.

Cold weather concreting (ACI 306R) is defined as periods when mean daily ambient temperature is below 40°F or the temperature is expected to fall below 40°F within 24 hours. Cold weather procedures include: preheating mix water (not to exceed the temperature that would cause flash set); preheating aggregates; using heated enclosures over the work area; specifying Type III cement or higher cement content for accelerated strength gain; and maintaining minimum concrete temperature of 50°F in the forms for the required protection period. The protection period depends on the element's mass, cement type, and specified strength — for standard concrete in typical section sizes, 7 days is a common minimum. The inspector must verify temperature monitoring during cold weather protection: temperature probes embedded in the concrete and read at 4-hour or 8-hour intervals are standard. A temperature log is required.

Structural Steel: Bolt Torquing and Weld Visual Inspection

High-Strength Bolt Installation: Methods and Verification

Structural steel connections rely on high-strength bolts — primarily ASTM A325 (now F3125 Grade A325) and A490 (F3125 Grade A490) — for the majority of field connections in industrial construction. These bolts work by developing high clamping force between the connected plies, which distributes load through friction at the faying surface and through bearing at the bolt hole. The specified pretension in these bolts is essential to connection performance; a bolt that is finger-tight or only snug-tight is not a proper high-strength structural bolt installation regardless of how it looks.

AISC 360 and the RCSC Specification for Structural Joints Using High-Strength Bolts together govern bolt installation for structural steel in the United States. Three installation methods are recognized: Turn-of-Nut, Tension Control (TC), and Direct Tension Indicator (DTI). The specification will identify which method is required and the inspector must be familiar with all three.

## Chapter 10 — Photo documentation

Photo Documentation: Scaling, Orientation, and The Context Shot

Photographs are the most powerful evidentiary tool available to the field inspector. They do what written description cannot — they place the viewer at the location and show them exactly what existed at a specific moment in time. A well-documented photograph proves that an inspector was present, that a specific condition existed, and that it existed at a specific location within the project. A poorly documented photograph is worse than no photograph, because it raises questions it cannot answer.

The Three-Shot Protocol

For every significant inspection point — and for every deficiency — document with three photographs. This is not a suggestion. It is a professional standard, and the three shots serve distinct purposes that are not interchangeable.

[]

Figure 10-2. The Three-Shot Photo Documentation Protocol. Context, intermediate, and close-up shots are required for every significant inspection point and every deficiency.

The context shot is a wide photograph taken from a distance sufficient to show the element being documented in relation to recognizable features of the structure. Column lines, floor levels, grid markers, permanent signage, cardinal directions — these are the landmarks that make the context shot work. The context shot answers the question "Where in the building is this?" Anyone reviewing the file months or years later — an attorney, an engineer, a claims adjuster — should be able to navigate to that exact location within the structure from the context shot alone. Without a context shot, a close-up photograph of a cracked weld is just a cracked weld somewhere. With it, that weld is in a specific location that can be found, re-examined, evaluated, and either repaired or accepted.

The intermediate shot moves in from the context shot to show the element and its surrounding conditions at medium distance. For a rebar inspection, the intermediate shot shows a ten-foot section of the reinforcing assembly — enough context to see how the element fits into the surrounding work. For a deficiency, the intermediate shot shows the defect and the conditions around it. This is the photograph that bridges the context shot and the close-up, allowing the viewer to understand the relationship between the specific detail and the broader construction element.

The close-up shot captures the specific detail that is the subject of the documentation. For a weld deficiency, this is the crack, the undercut, or the incomplete fusion. For a concrete test, this is the slump cone with the ruler in the frame. For a rebar dimension verification, this is the bar with the measuring tape extended alongside it. The close-up shot must contain enough detail to support the technical observation — a photograph of a surface crack in which the crack is not clearly visible is not documentation.

Scaling Requirements

A photograph of a defect without a scale reference is nearly useless for technical documentation. The viewer has no way to judge the size of the defect, which is typically the most important technical parameter. This is a failure that the inspector can never correct after the fact, because the opportunity to include a scale is only available at the time the photograph is taken.

Include a scale in every technical close-up photograph. A tape measure extended alongside or beneath the element is the most common and most credible scale because it shows actual dimensions rather than implying them. A coin — a U.S. quarter is approximately one inch in diameter — provides scale when a tape measure is not practical. A photographer's scale card, available from engineering supply stores and in several standard sizes, is ideal for small-scale defect documentation because it includes multiple measurement references and typically a color reference as well. For structural elements, a person in the photograph provides scale for large contexts, but should not substitute for a measuring device in technical documentation.

Photo Naming, Metadata, and Filing

A photograph that cannot be located in the project record is worthless. Establish a consistent photo naming convention at project mobilization and maintain it throughout the project. A functional convention includes the date, a sequence number, and a brief description: "2026-03-17_0142_Column-C7-Rebar-Inspection.jpg." The date is first so that chronological sorting is automatic. The sequence number distinguishes multiple photographs taken on the same day. The description identifies the subject without requiring the viewer to open the file.

Do not alter photograph metadata after the fact. Modern digital cameras and smartphones embed metadata — date, time, GPS coordinates, and camera settings — directly in the image file. This metadata is increasingly significant in construction disputes because it provides objective, camera-generated evidence of when and where a photograph was taken. An inspector's photograph with GPS coordinates that place it at the project site on the date claimed is more credible than one with no location data or inconsistent metadata.

Maintain photographs in a project-specific archive with folder structure that mirrors the ITP — organized by discipline, DFOW, and ITP line item. At closeout, the photo archive becomes part of the quality record and may be contractually required to be delivered to the owner. An archive of 3,000 photographs with consistent naming and organization is a deliverable. An archive of 3,000 photographs named "IMG_4521.jpg" through "IMG_7520.jpg" is a data liability.
